One order · two screens · zero refresh

Orvexa is a QR ordering and operations platform for restaurants in Tunisia. A diner scans the table, orders in Arabic, French or English, and the kitchen, the waiter and the owner all see it the same second - on four connected apps, with no app to install and no paper ticket. I designed and built it alone, end to end, over four months: product, brand, design system, frontends, realtime backend and the production infrastructure it runs on.

(My Role)

  • Designed the product and the “Saffron Hospitality” design system, from three moodboard directions to the final tokens.
  • Built the four frontends - customer, kitchen, waiter, admin - and the shared types and API client in one TypeScript monorepo.
  • Built the realtime API: orders, sessions, tables, payments and waiter calls over Socket.io rooms, backed by Postgres and Redis.
  • Took it to production: Docker, nginx with automatic HTTPS, backups, monitoring, runbooks - and the landing page, plans and contracts to sell it.

(The Problem)

In most restaurants the order still travels by paper: a waiter writes it, walks it to the kitchen, a printer jams, a ticket gets lost, and the table waves for someone who doesn't see them. The owner learns how the day went after it's over.

(The Bet)

One synced system instead of four disconnected habits. The diner orders from the table, the kitchen sees the ticket the second it's placed, the waiter sees who is calling and for how long, and the owner sees revenue, covers and what's selling live. No app to install, no diner account, no data kept after the meal.

(The Outcome)

Orvexa shipped to production on its own domain and went live with its first client, Capitol Amri, with their full menu, tables and staff in the system. Building it was the part I could do alone; selling it restaurant by restaurant was a different job. After a while Capitol went back to paper menus, and I paused the product.

(What I Learned)

  • Distribution is half the product. Four months of building, and the hardest problem was never in the code.
  • Habits beat features. A paper menu has no learning curve - adoption needs on-site onboarding and staff buy-in, not just a better screen.
  • Sell the smallest step first. Next time I'd start with the QR menu alone, prove it in a few restaurants, then grow into ordering and the kitchen display.

Process

01

Direction

Three moodboards - editorial, tech-crisp and hospitality-soft. Saffron won: warm and North-African without going terracotta. The working name was TableFlow; the product launched as Orvexa.

02

System

One token file for color, type, spacing, radius, shadow and motion, plus twelve primitives - so four very different screens still feel like one product.

03

Four Surfaces

Each user gets a screen built for their device and their pressure: a calm phone menu, a loud kitchen tablet, a one-hand waiter app and a dense owner dashboard.

04

Realtime Core

Orders, calls and table states push through Socket.io rooms per restaurant, table, kitchen and waiter. No polling, no refresh, and every non-happy path has a designed state.

05

Ship & Operate

Production on Docker Compose behind nginx with Let's Encrypt, nightly backups, health checks, runbooks for bad days - and a landing page and plans to sell it.

(Part 05 · Design System)

Saffron Hospitality.

One confident accent, warm paper instead of stark white, and an italic serif for the moments of hospitality - set against a precise sans for everything that has to be read fast.

(01 · Direction)

Three directions, explored side by side. Editorial was too quiet for a kitchen, tech-crisp too cold for a dining room. Saffron worked for both.

(02 · Foundations)

Warm-paper neutrals and one saffron scale, with dark operator surfaces for the kitchen. Newsreader italic for hospitality moments, Geist for everything read at speed.

(03 · Components & Brand)

Twelve primitives shared by all four apps, and a mark built from the same three tones - ink, paper and saffron.

(04 · Every State, Designed)

Real service isn't the happy path. Loading, empty, offline and failed payments each got a screen of their own - calm, specific, and always with a next step.

(Part 06 · Under the Hood)

Built to run a real service.

A TypeScript monorepo: four React 19 apps and one Express API sharing types, zod schemas and socket contracts - so a change in an order's shape breaks the build, not a dinner service.
